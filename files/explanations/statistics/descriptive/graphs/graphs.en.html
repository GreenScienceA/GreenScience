📈 Graphical Representation: When Numbers Learn to Speak

On the morning of January 28, 1986, the Space Shuttle Challenger broke apart 73 seconds after launch, killing all seven crew members. The night before, engineers at Morton Thiokol had desperately argued against launching in freezing temperatures, presenting thirteen charts to NASA managers showing that the O-ring seals in the solid rocket boosters had experienced problems at low temperatures. NASA overruled them and authorized launch. After the disaster, the physicist Richard Feynman and the information designer Edward Tufte independently analyzed the pre-launch charts and reached a devastating conclusion: the data clearly predicted the failure — but the engineers' graphs were so poorly designed that the pattern was invisible. Had the data been plotted in a simple scatter plot — O-ring damage versus temperature — the lethal trend would have been unmistakable (Tufte, 1997). Seven people died, in part, because of a bad graph.

This is neither exaggeration nor rhetoric. It is the most consequential lesson in the history of data visualization: how you display data can be a matter of life and death. A good graph does not merely decorate a research paper or brighten a presentation — it reveals patterns that tables of numbers hide, it persuades decision-makers to act, and it exposes truths that text alone cannot convey. Roughly 30% of the human cerebral cortex is devoted to visual processing, compared to just 8% for touch and 3% for hearing (Ware, 2012). When we convert a column of numbers into a well-crafted image, we are tapping into the most powerful information-processing system the human brain possesses.

This explanation is a comprehensive guide to the graphical tools of descriptive statistics — from the humble bar chart to the elegant box plot — designed not merely to teach you how to construct each graph, but why each one exists, when to use it, what it reveals and hides, and how to avoid the traps that turn data visualization from illumination into deception.

💡 The foundational insight: « The greatest value of a picture is when it forces us to notice what we never expected to see. » — John Tukey (1977, p. vi). Every graph is a visual argument. Like any argument, it can be brilliant, misleading, or incomprehensible depending on how it is constructed. The goal of this explanation is to ensure yours are always the first.

🏛️ Part I — A Brief History: The Invention of Statistical Graphics

Statistical graphics are so ubiquitous today that it is easy to forget how recently they were invented. For most of human history — thousands of years of trade, taxation, census-taking, and scientific measurement — numerical data was communicated exclusively through tables and prose. The visual display of quantitative information is barely 250 years old, and its history is inseparable from the stories of a handful of extraordinary individuals (Friendly, 2008).

William Playfair (1759–1823), a Scottish engineer and political economist with no formal mathematical training, single-handedly invented nearly every major chart type we use today. In his Commercial and Political Atlas (1786), he introduced the line graph — 43 time-series charts tracking England's trade balances — and the bar chart (a single example showing Scotland's imports and exports). In his Statistical Breviary (1801), he published the first pie chart (showing the proportions of the Turkish Empire's territories in Europe, Asia, and Africa) and the first circle graph (comparing the areas, populations, and revenues of European nations). Before Playfair, virtually no one had thought to represent numbers as shapes. His innovations were initially dismissed — contemporaries considered tables superior — but Playfair's visual vocabulary eventually became universal (Spence, 2005).

Half a century later, Florence Nightingale (1858) demonstrated the persuasive power of graphs when she presented her famous polar-area diagram — often called the "coxcomb" — to members of Parliament. The diagram showed that during the Crimean War, far more British soldiers died from preventable diseases (typhus, cholera, dysentery) than from battlefield wounds — a pattern that was invisible in the numerical tables but unmistakable in her visualization. Her graph drove sanitary reform across the British military medical system and saved countless lives, proving that a well-crafted graph can literally change the course of public policy (Magnello, 2012). Nightingale was elected the first female member of the Royal Statistical Society in 1859, in recognition of her contributions.

Perhaps the greatest single data visualization in history appeared in 1869, when the French civil engineer Charles Joseph Minard published his map of Napoleon's 1812 Russian campaign. On a single page, Minard displayed six variables simultaneously — the size of the army, its geographic location, the direction of travel, the temperature, the latitude and longitude, and the dates — creating an image so powerful that Tufte called it "probably the best statistical graphic ever drawn" (Tufte, 1983, p. 40). The width of the flowing band shrinks from 422,000 soldiers departing to a mere 10,000 returning — a visual elegy for the catastrophic toll of war.

In the 20th century, John Tukey (1977) revolutionized the field with his concept of Exploratory Data Analysis (EDA) — the philosophy that statisticians should look at their data before testing hypotheses, using visual tools to discover patterns, anomalies, and surprises. Tukey invented the stem-and-leaf plot and the box-and-whisker plot, both of which remain staples of modern statistics. And Edward Tufte (1983) established the theoretical principles of graphical excellence in his landmark work The Visual Display of Quantitative Information — a book that has influenced every serious data visualization since, from scientific journals to The New York Times.

🔬 Part II — Why Graphs Matter: Anscombe's Quartet

In 1973, the statistician Francis Anscombe published a paper that permanently changed how statisticians think about data visualization. He constructed four small datasets — each containing 11 (x, y) pairs — that share identical summary statistics: the same mean of x (9.0), the same mean of y (~7.5), the same variance of x (11.0), the same variance of y (~4.12), the same correlation (0.816), and the same regression line (y ≈ 3.0 + 0.5x). By every numerical measure, the four datasets are indistinguishable (Anscombe, 1973).

Yet when you plot them, the four datasets tell radically different stories. Dataset I shows a classic linear relationship. Dataset II reveals a perfect curvilinear (quadratic) pattern — the linear model is completely wrong. Dataset III contains a perfect linear trend with a single influential outlier that warps the regression line. Dataset IV shows a vertical cluster with one extreme point that alone creates the illusion of correlation. No table of summary statistics could possibly reveal these differences — only a graph can.

Anscombe's Quartet delivers a message that every student of statistics must internalize: never trust summary statistics alone. Always plot your data first. As Anscombe himself wrote: "Graphs are essential to good statistical analysis... both calculations and graphs should be studied; neither alone is sufficient" (Anscombe, 1973, p. 17). More recently, Matejka and Fitzmaurice (2017) extended this idea with the "Datasaurus Dozen" — thirteen datasets that share the same statistical properties but form shapes ranging from a star to a dinosaur when plotted, a vivid 21st-century restatement of Anscombe's principle.

🧪 The Anscombe Principle: If someone presents you with a mean, a standard deviation, and a correlation but no graph, you know almost nothing about the data. Summary statistics are a compression — they discard shape, outliers, clusters, gaps, and patterns. Graphs restore what the numbers erase.

🧭 Part III — Choosing the Right Graph

Not all graphs are interchangeable. The type of graph you choose depends on two critical factors: the nature of your variable (qualitative or quantitative, discrete or continuous) and the analytical question you are trying to answer. Choosing the wrong graph is like translating poetry into a language that has no word for "beauty" — you may produce something, but the meaning is lost (Moore et al., 2021).

📊 Part IV — Bar Charts: The Workhorses of Categorical Data

What They Are

A bar chart (or bar graph) uses rectangular bars of equal width to represent the frequency (or relative frequency, or percentage) of each category. The height (or length, in horizontal bar charts) of each bar is proportional to the value it represents. The bars are separated by gaps — this is the critical visual cue that distinguishes bar charts from histograms and signals that the categories are discrete and independent. The order of bars is generally arbitrary for nominal data but fixed for ordinal data (Agresti & Franklin, 2018).

When to Use Them

Bar charts are ideal for qualitative (categorical) variables — blood types, land-use categories, disease classifications, survey responses, crop varieties — and for discrete quantitative variables with a small number of values (e.g., number of children per family). They answer the question: How do different categories compare in frequency or magnitude?

Construction rules: ① Draw horizontal axis (categories) and vertical axis (frequency or %). ② Each bar has the same width. ③ Leave equal gaps between bars — signaling discrete categories. ④ Bar height = frequency (or relative frequency) of that category. ⑤ Label both axes clearly and add an informative title.

Key variants: Grouped (clustered) bar charts place bars side-by-side within each category to compare subgroups (e.g., cause of death by country). Stacked bar charts stack segments within each bar to show composition while preserving the total. Pareto charts — named after the Italian economist Vilfredo Pareto and popularized by quality engineer Joseph Juran (1951) — sort bars in descending order of frequency and overlay a cumulative percentage line, embodying the "80/20 principle" that a few categories often account for most of the total.

🔑 Common error: Never connect the tops of bars in a bar chart with a line — this implies continuity and interpolation between categories, which is meaningless for nominal data. Lines connecting categories suggest that intermediate values exist between "Cancer" and "Diabetes," which is nonsensical.

🥧 Part V — Pie Charts: Parts of a Whole

What They Are

A pie chart is a circular diagram divided into slices (sectors), where the angle (and thus the area) of each slice is proportional to the relative frequency of that category. Since a full circle spans 360°, a category with relative frequency fᵢ occupies an angle of fᵢ × 360°. Invented by William Playfair in 1801, it remains one of the most recognized — and most debated — chart types in statistics (Freedman et al., 2007; Spence, 2005).

When to Use Them (and When NOT to)

Pie charts are effective for showing part-to-whole relationships when you have a small number of categories (ideally 2–6) and the primary message is about proportions rather than precise comparisons. They answer: What share of the total does each category represent?

However, pie charts are widely criticized by statisticians and visualization researchers. Edward Tufte (1983) argued that pie charts are "a poor method of comparison because of the difficulty of judging areas and angles." Cleveland and McGill (1984) demonstrated experimentally that people estimate quantities far more accurately from position along a common scale (bar charts) than from angle or area (pie charts) — a finding replicated across numerous perceptual studies. Heer and Bostock (2010) further confirmed that arc length judgments in pie charts are systematically biased, especially for slices near 50%.

⚠️ The pie chart debate: Tufte (1983) wrote that pie charts should be replaced by tables or bar charts in nearly all cases. Cleveland (1985) concurred. Yet pie charts remain enormously popular in executive dashboards, media reporting, and public health communication because they powerfully convey the intuitive idea of "shares of a whole." The compromise: use them sparingly, limit to 2–6 slices, always label with percentages, and never use 3D pie charts — the tilted perspective distorts angles and makes accurate comparison impossible. A doughnut chart (pie with a hollow center) performs equally poorly on perceptual accuracy but can accommodate a central label.

📶 Part VI — Histograms: The Heart of Quantitative Data

What They Are

The histogram is the single most important graphical tool in descriptive statistics. It displays the frequency distribution of a continuous quantitative variable using adjacent rectangular bars, where each bar covers a class interval (bin) and its height represents the frequency (or relative frequency, or frequency density) of observations falling within that interval. Crucially, the bars touch each other — there are no gaps — because the data is continuous and the class intervals are contiguous. The histogram makes the shape of a distribution visible: its symmetry or skewness, its modality (unimodal, bimodal, multimodal), its spread, and the presence of gaps or outliers (Wackerly et al., 2014).

Histogram vs. Bar Chart: The Critical Difference

Students frequently confuse histograms and bar charts. The differences are fundamental, not cosmetic:

🔍 Reading the histogram: From this histogram you can instantly see that the distribution is unimodal (one clear peak), the peak is at [20, 25[ cm (the modal class), the distribution is slightly right-skewed (the tail extends further to the right than to the left), and most fish are between 15 and 30 cm. None of these patterns are as immediately visible from the table alone — this is precisely why histograms exist.

How Many Bins? The Binning Problem

The appearance of a histogram depends critically on the number of class intervals (bins). Too few bins produce an over-smoothed picture that hides features; too many bins produce a spiky, noisy picture where random variation obscures the underlying pattern. Several guidelines exist: Sturges' rule (1926): k = 1 + 3.322 log₁₀(n); the square root rule: k ≈ √n; and Freedman-Diaconis rule (1981): bin width = 2 × IQR × n⁻¹/³, which adapts to the data's spread. For n = 50, Sturges' rule gives k ≈ 7 bins, the square root rule gives k ≈ 7, and the Freedman-Diaconis rule adapts to the actual IQR. In practice, try several bin widths and choose the one that best reveals the distribution's shape without excessive noise (Freedman & Diaconis, 1981; Scott, 1979).

⚠️ Special Case: Unequal Class Widths

When class widths are not equal — common in datasets where some intervals are wider to capture sparse tails — using frequency as the bar height produces a misleading histogram because wider bars appear to contain more data simply by being taller. In this case, you must use frequency density on the y-axis: Frequency density = nᵢ / class width. This ensures that the area (not the height) of each bar is proportional to the frequency, preserving the fundamental principle that in a histogram, area represents count (Freedman et al., 2007).

📉 Part VII — Frequency Polygons: Connecting the Dots

What They Are

A frequency polygon is constructed by plotting a point at the midpoint (class mark) of each class interval at a height equal to the frequency, then connecting adjacent points with straight line segments. The polygon is "closed" by extending it to the midpoints of hypothetical empty classes at each end — ensuring the enclosed area exactly equals the total area of the corresponding histogram. This geometric identity holds because each triangle "gained" above a bar is offset by a triangle "lost" below it — a beautiful property that makes the frequency polygon area-equivalent to the histogram (Wackerly et al., 2014; Moore et al., 2021).

Why Use a Frequency Polygon Instead of a Histogram?

Frequency polygons have two decisive advantages: ① They allow multiple distributions to be overlaid on the same graph — overlapping histograms become unreadable beyond two groups, but overlapping polygons remain clear even for three or four distributions. This makes them ideal for comparing the length distributions of fish across different seasons, or exam score distributions across different classes. ② They emphasize the continuity and flow of the distribution rather than the blocky appearance of individual bars, giving a clearer sense of the distribution's overall shape.

Construction from the Fish Data

① Add a hypothetical class [5, 10[ with frequency 0 before the first class. ② Add a hypothetical class [40, 45[ with frequency 0 after the last class. ③ Plot points at each midpoint: (7.5, 0), (12.5, 4), (17.5, 9), (22.5, 16), (27.5, 12), (32.5, 6), (37.5, 3), (42.5, 0). ④ Connect adjacent points with straight line segments. The polygon touches the x-axis at both ends, enclosing an area equal to the histogram.

💡 Key property: The total area under the frequency polygon equals the total area of the corresponding histogram. This is not a coincidence but a mathematical necessity — and it is why the frequency polygon is a valid alternative representation of the same distributional information. When both are displayed together (polygon overlaying histogram), the visual relationship is immediately apparent.

📈 Part VIII — Cumulative Frequency Curve (Ogive): The Running Total

What It Is

An ogive (pronounced oh-JIVE, from the architectural term for a pointed arch) is a graph of the cumulative frequency (or cumulative relative frequency) plotted against the upper class boundary of each interval. The resulting S-shaped curve always rises monotonically from zero to N (or from 0% to 100%). It is constructed by: ① starting at the lower boundary of the first class with cumulative frequency = 0; ② plotting a point at each upper class boundary showing the total count of all observations up to and including that class; ③ connecting the points with straight line segments or a smooth curve (Gravetter & Wallnau, 2017).

What It Reveals

The ogive is uniquely powerful for answering cumulative questions — questions about proportions above, below, or between specific values: "What percentage of fish are shorter than 25 cm?" — read horizontally from x = 25 to the curve, then vertically to the y-axis. "What length is exceeded by only 25% of fish?" — this is Q₃: read from y = 75% to the curve, then down to the x-axis. The median is where the curve crosses the 50% line. Quartiles Q₁ and Q₃ are where it crosses the 25% and 75% lines. The interquartile range (IQR) is the horizontal distance between Q₁ and Q₃ on the x-axis. Any percentile can be read by the same method. The slope of the ogive at any point indicates the density of observations — steep sections mean high concentration, flat sections mean sparse data.

🌿 Part IX — Stem-and-Leaf Plots: The Best of Both Worlds

What They Are

Invented by John Tukey (1977) as part of his Exploratory Data Analysis revolution, the stem-and-leaf plot (or stemplot) is a semi-graphical device that displays the distribution shape (like a histogram rotated 90°) while preserving every individual data value. Each observation is split into a "stem" (the leading digit or digits) and a "leaf" (the trailing digit). The stems are listed vertically in ascending order, and the leaves extend horizontally to the right — forming a sideways histogram where no information is lost. This dual character — part table, part graph — is what makes the stem-and-leaf plot unique: you can see the shape of the distribution and read off every single observation (Tukey, 1977).

Worked Example

Scenario: Final exam scores (out of 100) for 30 students in an introductory statistics course at a university in São Paulo, Brazil:

Construction rules: ① Choose the stem unit (here: tens digit) and leaf unit (units digit). ② List stems vertically from smallest to largest. ③ Write each leaf next to its stem, in ascending order. ④ Include a key: "Stem 7 | Leaf 3 = 73." ⑤ Optionally, add a frequency column.

🔑 What you see instantly: The distribution is roughly symmetric and bell-shaped, with the highest concentration in the 70s (8 students). The lowest score is 42, the highest 96. The median (average of 15th and 16th values) = (71 + 72)/2 = 71.5. You can read every single score from the display — something no histogram can offer. This is why Tukey called stem-and-leaf displays a tool for "detective work" on data.

Variants and Extensions

Split stems: When a stem has too many leaves (more than ~15), split each stem into two lines — one for leaves 0–4, one for 5–9 — doubling the resolution. Back-to-back stem-and-leaf plots: Two datasets can be compared using a shared stem, with one group's leaves extending to the left and the other's to the right — ideal for comparing exam scores of two sections, heights of males versus females, or yields of two crop varieties (Gravetter & Wallnau, 2017). Limitation: Stem-and-leaf plots become impractical for large datasets (n > 100–150), where histograms or density plots are preferable.

📦 Part X — Box-and-Whisker Plots: The Five-Number Summary Made Visual

What They Are

The box plot (or box-and-whisker plot), invented by John Tukey (1977) and refined by McGill, Tukey, and Larsen (1978), is one of the most information-dense graphical tools in all of statistics. In a single compact figure, it displays five key statistics simultaneously — the minimum (or lower fence), first quartile (Q₁), median (Q₂), third quartile (Q₃), and maximum (or upper fence) — plus any outliers plotted as individual points. From this display, you can read the center (median), the spread (IQR and range), the skewness (asymmetry of box and whiskers), and the presence of unusual observations — all at a glance (McGill et al., 1978).

Anatomy of a Box Plot

ComponentRepresentsWhat It Tells You

The 1.5 × IQR rule for defining outliers was proposed by Tukey (1977) and has become the standard convention. Points beyond 3 × IQR from the box are sometimes called extreme outliers, distinguished from "mild" outliers between 1.5 and 3 × IQR. The choice of 1.5 was not arbitrary — for a perfectly normal distribution, the inner fences capture approximately 99.3% of the data, meaning about 0.7% would be flagged as potential outliers (Hoaglin et al., 1986).

Worked Example

Using the 30 exam scores: Min = 42, Q₁ = 58.5, Median = 71.5, Q₃ = 84.5, Max = 96.

IQR = 84.5 − 58.5 = 26. Lower fence = 58.5 − 1.5(26) = 19.5 → since Min = 42 > 19.5, lower whisker extends to 42. Upper fence = 84.5 + 1.5(26) = 123.5 → since Max = 96 < 123.5, upper whisker extends to 96. No outliers in this dataset.

🔍 Reading the box plot: The median line sits slightly below center within the box, and the lower whisker (42 to 58.5 = 16.5 units) is slightly shorter than the upper whisker (84.5 to 96 = 11.5 units). The box itself is relatively wide (IQR = 26), indicating substantial variability. The overall picture suggests a roughly symmetric distribution with a slight right skew.

Side-by-Side Box Plots: The True Power

The box plot's greatest strength emerges in comparison. Placing box plots side by side — for instance, comparing patient recovery times across four hospitals, or crop yields across five fertilizer treatments, or student performance across three teaching methods — allows immediate visual comparison of location, spread, skewness, and outliers across groups. This is why box plots are the default visualization in experimental biology, clinical trials, and agricultural research. Krzywinski and Altman (2014), writing in Nature Methods, recommended that "box plots should be the standard for displaying grouped continuous data" in scientific publications because they reveal distributional information that bar charts of means with error bars systematically conceal.

📉 Part XI — Line Graphs: Change Over Time

What They Are

A line graph displays data points connected by straight line segments, with the independent variable (usually time) on the x-axis and the dependent variable on the y-axis. First invented by William Playfair (1786), who used them to track England's trade balances over decades, line graphs are the natural choice for visualizing trends, cycles, seasonal patterns, and temporal evolution. The line itself is data — its slope encodes the rate of change between consecutive points. A steep line means rapid change; a flat line means stability; a changing slope reveals acceleration or deceleration (Tufte, 1983).

When to Use Them

Line graphs are appropriate only when the x-axis represents an ordered quantitative variable — most commonly time (days, months, years), but also dose, age, or distance. They are inappropriate for categorical data: connecting "Male" to "Female" with a line implies a continuum that does not exist. Multiple lines on the same axes enable powerful trend comparisons — for instance, tracking GDP growth across several countries, or comparing monthly temperatures across different cities.

Construction Principles

Scenario: Monthly average temperature (°C) in Reykjavík, Iceland, over one year: Jan −0.5, Feb −0.2, Mar 0.9, Apr 3.4, May 7.1, Jun 9.8, Jul 11.2, Aug 10.8, Sep 7.4, Oct 3.9, Nov 1.2, Dec −0.1.

① X-axis: time (months) with equal spacing. ② Y-axis: temperature in °C, starting at a value that includes the minimum. ③ Plot each point at the intersection of month and temperature. ④ Connect consecutive points with straight line segments. ⑤ The resulting line reveals the seasonal cycle immediately — the sinusoidal rise from winter to summer and back.

💡 Design principle: Avoid "spaghetti graphs" — line graphs with so many overlapping lines that no individual trend is discernible. If you have more than 5–6 groups, consider small multiples (individual panels for each group with identical scales) rather than a single crowded graph. Tufte (1983) called small multiples "the best design solution for a wide range of problems in data presentation."

🔵 Part XII — Scatter Plots: Exploring Relationships

What They Are

A scatter plot (or scatter diagram) displays the relationship between two quantitative variables by plotting each observation as a point in a two-dimensional coordinate system. The independent variable (or the one hypothesized to be explanatory) is placed on the x-axis, and the dependent variable (or response) on the y-axis. The resulting "cloud" of points reveals the direction (positive, negative, or no association), form (linear or curvilinear), strength (tight or dispersed cloud), and outliers (points far from the general pattern) (Cleveland, 1985).

Worked Example

Scenario: A researcher records hours of study (x) and exam score (y) for 15 university students in Seoul, South Korea. Plotting the 15 points reveals a moderately strong positive linear trend — students who studied more tended to score higher — with one notable outlier: a student who studied 12 hours but scored only 55, well below the trend, suggesting an unusual circumstance (illness, test anxiety, or measurement error) that warrants investigation.

What to look for in a scatter plot: ① Direction: upward trend (positive), downward (negative), or no pattern. ② Form: does the cloud follow a straight line (linear) or a curve (quadratic, exponential, logarithmic)? ③ Strength: how tightly do the points cluster around the underlying form? A tight cloud means a strong relationship. ④ Outliers: any points that deviate markedly from the general pattern.

🔑 Critical reminder: Scatter plots show association, not causation. A strong visual pattern does not prove that one variable causes changes in the other. The classic example: ice cream sales and drowning deaths are strongly positively correlated — not because ice cream causes drowning, but because both increase with temperature. « Correlation is not causation » is perhaps the single most important principle in all of statistics (Freedman et al., 2007).

🎨 Part XIII — Tufte's Principles of Graphical Excellence

In 1983, Edward Tufte published The Visual Display of Quantitative Information — a work that established the theoretical foundations for evaluating statistical graphics. His principles remain the gold standard against which all data visualizations are judged:

Maximize the data-ink ratioThe proportion of ink devoted to non-redundant display of data should approach 1.0Remove unnecessary gridlines, borders, backgrounds, decorations — every drop of ink should convey information

The Lie Factor

Tufte defined the Lie Factor as: Lie Factor = (size of effect shown in graphic) / (size of effect in data). A Lie Factor of 1.0 represents perfect honesty. Values above 1.05 or below 0.95 indicate distortion — whether intentional or accidental. Tufte documented numerous real-world examples where Lie Factors exceeded 2.0 or 3.0, particularly in government reports and journalistic graphics where 3D effects, truncated axes, and area-based encodings inflated small differences into dramatic visual effects (Tufte, 1983).

🎯 Tufte's summary: « Graphical excellence is the well-designed presentation of interesting data — a matter of substance, of statistics, and of design... Graphical excellence consists of complex ideas communicated with clarity, precision, and efficiency » (Tufte, 1983, p. 51). The best graph is not the prettiest — it is the one that most honestly and efficiently reveals what the data has to say.

🚨 Part XIV — Graphical Deception: How Graphs Lie

If graphs can illuminate, they can also deceive — and the history of data visualization is littered with examples of charts designed (or accidentally constructed) to mislead. Alberto Cairo's How Charts Lie (2019) catalogued the most common techniques of graphical deception:

Deception TechniqueHow It WorksReal-World Example
Truncated y-axisStarting the y-axis above zero exaggerates small differences into dramatic visual gapsA 2% change made to look like a 50% change in corporate earnings reports
Aspect ratio manipulationStretching or compressing the graph alters the apparent slope of trendsA gradual warming trend made to look flat (or dramatic) by adjusting width-to-height ratio
3D effectsAdding depth distorts areas, angles, and distances3D pie charts where front slices appear larger than back slices of equal value
Cherry-picked time rangeShowing only the period that supports the desired narrativeShowing stock performance only during a rally, hiding the preceding crash
Dual y-axesTwo different scales on left and right can make unrelated trends appear correlatedSpurious correlation graphics pairing unrelated variables (e.g., margarine consumption and divorce rate)
Area/volume encodingScaling a 2D or 3D object by its linear dimension squares or cubes the visual effectA bar twice as tall and twice as wide represents 4× the visual area but only 2× the data
⚠️ The critical consumer's checklist: When reading any graph, ask: ① Does the y-axis start at zero? If not, is the break clearly marked? ② Are the axes labeled with units? ③ Is the time range or sample appropriate? ④ Are 3D effects distorting proportions? ⑤ Is the Lie Factor close to 1.0? A statistically literate citizen is a graph-literate citizen — and graph literacy is a form of self-defense against manipulation (Cairo, 2019).

⚠️ Part XV — Common Mistakes in Graphical Representation

MistakeWhy It's WrongHow to Fix It
Histogram for categorical dataAdjacent bars imply nonexistent continuity between categoriesUse a bar chart (with gaps)
Bar chart for continuous dataGaps between bars imply discrete categories when data is continuousUse a histogram (no gaps)
Truncating the y-axis without break markExaggerates small differences; distorts perception of changeStart y-axis at zero or clearly mark the break with a zigzag symbol
Using 3D effectsDistorts areas, angles, and distances — violates graphical integrityAlways use 2D graphics for statistical data
Too many pie slices (>6)Human perception cannot distinguish small angular differencesGroup small categories into "Other" or switch to a bar chart
Missing labels, titles, or unitsGraph becomes uninterpretable — violates self-sufficiency principleAlways label both axes with variable names and units; add an informative title
Connecting categorical data points with linesImplies interpolation and continuity between discrete categoriesUse bars for categories; reserve lines for ordered quantitative data
Ignoring outliers in box plotsMisses potentially important anomalies, errors, or discoveriesInvestigate every outlier: is it a data entry error, a measurement problem, or a genuine extreme value?
Using frequency (not density) with unequal binsWider bins appear inflated — area no longer proportional to countUse frequency density = nᵢ / class width on the y-axis

🧪 Part XVI — One Dataset, Many Views: A Synthesis

Each graphical tool reveals different aspects of the same data. No single graph tells the complete story — which is why Tukey (1977) insisted on looking at data from every possible angle:

GraphWhat It RevealsWhat It HidesBest For
Bar chartCategory comparisons, frequency rankingDistribution shape, individual valuesCategorical / discrete data
Pie chartPart-to-whole proportionsPrecise comparisons, many categories2–6 categories, proportions message
HistogramDistribution shape, modal class, skewness, gapsIndividual values, exact quartilesContinuous data, single distribution
Frequency polygonShape, flow, multi-group comparisonLess intuitive for single distributionsOverlaying 2–4 distributions
OgiveCumulative proportions, median, quartiles, percentilesDistribution shape less directly visiblePositional statistics, percentile questions
Stem-and-leafShape + every individual data valueImpractical for n > 100–150Small datasets, EDA, teaching
Box plotFive-number summary, outliers, skewness, group comparisonModality (bimodal hidden), shape detailsComparing groups, detecting outliers
Line graphTrends, cycles, rate of change over timeDistribution of values at each time pointTime-series data
Scatter plotRelationship direction, form, strength, outliersIndividual variable distributionsTwo quantitative variables
💡 The master analyst's approach: Never rely on a single graph. As Tukey (1977) taught, exploratory data analysis means examining data from every angle. Each graph reveals something different — the histogram shows shape, the box plot shows summary statistics and outliers, the stem-and-leaf preserves individual values, the scatter plot reveals relationships. Together, and only together, they tell the complete story. The best analysts are those who habitually ask: « What might I be missing? Let me try another view. »
