⚖️ Mass-Volume Relations: The Quantitative Architecture of Soil

In 1927, a Swedish agronomist named Albert Atterberg — the same scientist who gave us the Atterberg limits for clay consistency — posed a deceptively simple question: how do you describe a soil completely using only mass and volume? His insight, refined over the following decades by the Danish physicist Edgar Buckingham and the Israeli soil physicist Daniel Hillel, was that every physical property of soil — its strength, its drainage, its thermal behaviour, its capacity to sustain roots — ultimately reduces to how mass and volume are distributed among three phases: solid, liquid, and gas. This is the mass-volume framework, and it is to soil physics what the periodic table is to chemistry: the organizing structure upon which everything else is built (Hillel, 2004).

To understand why this matters, consider a practical problem. An irrigation engineer in the Mitidja Plain south of Algiers must decide how much water to apply to a wheat field. Too little, and the crop suffers drought stress. Too much, and the waterlogged soil deprives roots of oxygen, promoting anaerobiosis and root rot. The answer depends entirely on knowing the current mass-volume state of the soil: how much of the soil volume is solid mineral, how much is pore space, how much of that pore space is already filled with water, and how much remains filled with air. Every irrigation schedule, every drainage design, every foundation calculation in geotechnical engineering, and every contamination transport model in environmental science begins here — with the mass-volume relations of soil (Jury & Horton, 2004).

💡 The Core Principle: Soil is a three-phase porous medium — a mixture of solid particles (minerals + organic matter), liquid (water + dissolved solutes), and gas (soil air). The mass-volume relations are the mathematical framework that quantifies the proportions, densities, and water status of these three phases. Master these relations, and you possess the quantitative foundation upon which all of soil physics, soil mechanics, hydrology, and agricultural engineering are constructed (Hillel, 2004; Brady & Weil, 2017).

🧊 Part I — The Three-Phase System: Conceptual Foundation

Every sample of soil, whether collected from the Saharan reg, the Tell Atlas forest floor, or a rice paddy in Bangladesh, is a three-phase system composed of four components distributed across three physical states:

PhaseComponentsTypical Volume FractionState
🪨 SolidMineral particles (quartz, feldspars, clay minerals, carbonates, iron oxides) + organic matter (humus, roots, organisms)45–65% of total volumeRigid framework — provides structure and chemical reactivity
💧 LiquidWater + dissolved substances (ions, organic acids, nutrients)35–55% of total volume (shared between liquid and gas — their ratio changes dynamically)Mobile phase — transport medium for nutrients and contaminants
💨 GasSoil air (≈78% N₂, 20.5% O₂, 0.5–5% CO₂, trace CH₄, N₂O, NH₃)Compressible phase — supplies O₂ for root respiration and soil organisms

The mental model is a sponge: the sponge material is the solid phase (fixed skeleton), and the holes (pores) are filled with either water or air depending on the moisture state. When you squeeze the sponge (compaction), total pore volume decreases, bulk density increases, and both water capacity and aeration suffer. When you release it (tillage, frost action, biological activity), pore volume recovers. This dynamic interplay between the three phases governs every physical process in the soil — from infiltration to heat conduction to gas exchange (Hillel, 2004).

The Block Diagram: Visualising Mass-Volume Relations

The standard pedagogical tool in soil physics is the block diagram (also called the phase diagram), first formalized by Karl Terzaghi in his 1925 foundation text Erdbaumechanik. The diagram represents a unit soil sample as a rectangle divided into three horizontal compartments — solids at the bottom, water in the middle, air at the top — with volumes on the left axis and masses on the right axis. This simple device makes the relationships between all mass-volume parameters immediately visible (Terzaghi et al., 1996).

Every mass-volume parameter in soil science is derived from combinations of these six quantities: Vt, Vs, Vl, Va, Ms, Ml. The entire edifice of soil physical characterization rests on measuring these six values accurately and computing their ratios (Jury & Horton, 2004).

🏗️ Part II — Soil Density: How Tightly Packed Is the Matrix?

Density — mass per unit volume — is the most fundamental physical measurement of any material. In soil science, we define four distinct density parameters, each answering a different question about the soil's physical state.

1. Bulk Density (ρb) — The Compaction Indicator

Bulk density is arguably the single most important physical property in applied soil science. It is the mass of oven-dry soil (dried at 105°C for 24 hours to remove all gravitational and capillary water) divided by the total volume of the undisturbed sample (solids + pores):

ρb = Ms / Vt   (units: g/cm³, kg/m³, or Mg/m³)

Bulk density integrates three soil attributes into one number: particle size distribution (texture), structural arrangement (aggregation), and degree of compaction. It is the bridge between mass-based and volume-based measurements — without ρb, you cannot convert gravimetric water content to volumetric water content, and you cannot calculate total water storage in a soil profile (Brady & Weil, 2017).

Soil Conditionρb (g/cm³)Interpretation
Organic/peat soils0.1–0.6Extremely low — very high porosity, dominated by organic matter (ρs ≈ 1.2–1.4 g/cm³)
Well-structured forest topsoil0.7–1.0Excellent — high biological activity, abundant macropores, root-friendly
Cultivated loam (good tilth)1.1–1.3Normal agricultural range — adequate porosity for crops
Sandy soils1.4–1.6Higher due to single-grain structure — large pores but fewer total pores
Compacted subsoil / plough pan1.5–1.8Root-limiting — tillage pans restrict root penetration and water movement
Glacial till / hardpan1.8–2.2Root-impenetrable — effectively a barrier layer
⚠️ Root-limiting threshold: For most agricultural crops, root growth is severely restricted when ρb exceeds 1.5–1.6 g/cm³ in clay soils and 1.7–1.8 g/cm³ in sandy soils. This threshold determines whether compacted fields require deep ripping or subsoiling — a critical management decision in Algerian cereal-growing regions like the Sétif and Constantine high plateaus where tractor traffic on wet soils causes persistent compaction (Hamza & Anderson, 2005).

Measurement methods: The core method (driving a steel cylinder of known volume into the soil, excavating it, and oven-drying) is the standard field technique. Alternatives include the clod method (coating a natural soil clod with paraffin wax, measuring displacement volume), the sand cone method (excavating a hole and filling it with calibrated sand to measure volume), and the gamma-ray attenuation method (non-destructive, used in nuclear moisture-density gauges) (Jury & Horton, 2004).

2. Wet Bulk Density (ρt) — The Field-State Density

The total mass (solids + water) divided by total volume — representing the soil as it exists in the field at its current moisture state:

ρt = (Ms + Mw) / Vt = ρb(1 + w)

Wet bulk density is essential for earthwork calculations in civil engineering — when a contractor excavates soil for a dam embankment, the mass of material moved depends on ρt, not ρb. Range: typically 1.0–2.2 g/cm³ depending on soil type and moisture state.

3. Particle Density (ρs) — The Mineral Fingerprint

The density of the solid particles themselves, with all pore space excluded — measured by dividing the mass of oven-dry solids by the volume occupied by the solid phase only:

ρs = Ms / Vs   (units: g/cm³)

Particle density is determined by mineral composition, not by soil structure or management. For most mineral soils, ρs falls within the narrow range of 2.60–2.75 g/cm³ because quartz (2.65 g/cm³) and feldspars (2.55–2.76 g/cm³) dominate the mineral fraction of most soils worldwide. The conventional default value ρs = 2.65 g/cm³ is used when direct measurement is unavailable (Hillel, 2004).

Measurement: The pycnometer method (also called volumetric flask method) is the standard laboratory technique. A known mass of oven-dry soil is placed in a calibrated flask, water is added to fill remaining volume, and Vs is determined by displacement. Gas pycnometers using helium (which penetrates even the finest pores) provide the most accurate results (Flint & Flint, 2002).

🔑 Critical distinction: Bulk density (ρb) changes with tillage, compaction, biological activity, wetting-drying cycles, and freeze-thaw cycles — it is a management-sensitive property. Particle density (ρs) is essentially fixed by mineral composition — no amount of farming changes it. This distinction is fundamental: ρb is the property you manage; ρs is the property you inherit.

4. Specific Gravity (Gs) — The Dimensionless Ratio

The ratio of particle density to the density of water at 4°C (ρw = 1.000 g/cm³ = 1,000 kg/m³):

Gs = ρs / ρw   (dimensionless; for most soils Gs ≈ 2.65)

Since ρw = 1.000 g/cm³ in CGS units, Gs is numerically identical to ρs expressed in g/cm³ — but without units. Specific gravity is used extensively in geotechnical engineering for calculating phase relations in earthwork specifications (ASTM D854) and in hydrometer analysis for particle size determination (Terzaghi et al., 1996).

5. Specific Volume (Vb) — The Reciprocal

Vb = 1 / ρb = Vt / Ms   (units: cm³/g)

The volume occupied by one gram of oven-dry soil, including its associated pore space. Useful for converting between mass-based and volume-based calculations. Range: 0.45–1.4 cm³/g. For a typical agricultural soil with ρb = 1.3 g/cm³, Vb = 0.77 cm³/g.

🕳️ Part III — Porosity: The Architecture of Empty Space

If density describes how much solid material is packed into a volume, porosity describes how much empty space remains. That empty space — the pore network — is where all the action happens: water flows through it, air circulates in it, roots grow into it, microorganisms colonize it. Without adequate porosity, soil becomes impenetrable stone. The great soil physicist L.A. Richards, who developed the pressure plate apparatus in 1941, wrote: « The pore space is the living space of the soil — its lungs, its blood vessels, and its highways » (Richards, 1954).

1. Total Porosity (P or n) — The Grand Total

The fraction of total soil volume occupied by pores (voids) — both water-filled and air-filled:

P = Vp / Vt = (Va + Vl) / Vt = 1 − (ρb / ρs)

The formula P = 1 − (ρb / ρs) is one of the most powerful equations in soil physics because it derives porosity from two easily measured densities. Using the standard assumption ρs = 2.65 g/cm³, a soil with ρb = 1.3 g/cm³ has P = 1 − (1.3/2.65) = 0.509 — about 51% pore space (Hillel, 2004).

Soil TypeP (approximate)Explanation
Coarse sand0.35–0.40Single-grain structure — large pores but low total porosity; rapid drainage
Sandy loam0.40–0.47Mixed structure — intermediate
Silt loam0.45–0.55Well-aggregated — good balance of macropores and micropores
Clay (compacted)0.40–0.45Dispersion and compaction reduce porosity despite small particles
Clay (well-structured)0.50–0.65Strong aggregation creates inter-aggregate macropores → high total porosity
Peat / organic soil0.75–0.95Extremely high — organic fibres create enormous pore volume
🧠 The porosity paradox: Clay soils have higher total porosity than sandy soils — yet they drain more slowly. Why? Because clay pores are predominantly micropores (< 0.05 mm) that hold water by capillary forces, while sandy pores are predominantly macropores (> 0.05 mm) that drain freely under gravity. Total porosity tells you how much space exists; pore size distribution tells you how that space behaves (Brady & Weil, 2017).

2. Void Ratio (e) — The Geotechnical Standard

e = Vp / Vs = P / (1 − P) = (ρs / ρb) − 1

Void ratio compares pore volume to solid volume rather than total volume. It is the preferred porosity measure in geotechnical engineering because Vs remains constant during consolidation (only Vp changes), making void ratio a more natural variable for tracking settlement and compression. Range: 0.3 (very dense sand) to 2.5+ (soft organic clay). A void ratio of 1.0 means pore volume equals solid volume — exactly 50% porosity (Terzaghi et al., 1996).

The conversion between P and e is: P = e / (1 + e) and e = P / (1 − P).

3. Air-Filled Porosity (Fa or na) — The Aeration Measure

Fa = Va / Vt = P − θ

Air-filled porosity is the fraction of total volume occupied by air — it represents the breathing capacity of the soil. This parameter carries a critical biological threshold:

⚠️ The 10% rule: When Fa drops below 0.10 (10%), oxygen diffusion becomes insufficient for root respiration and aerobic microbial activity. Roots enter anaerobiosis — switching from aerobic to anaerobic metabolism, which produces toxic ethanol and acetaldehyde, eventually killing the root tips. This threshold explains why waterlogged soils kill most crop plants within 24–72 hours, why rice (Oryza sativa) requires specialized aerenchyma tissue to survive in flooded paddies, and why drainage engineering exists as an entire discipline (Hillel, 2004; Jury & Horton, 2004).

4. Air Ratio (ar)

ar = Va / Vs

Air volume relative to solid volume — analogous to void ratio but for the air phase only. Used in expansive clay soil mechanics where volume changes must be tracked relative to a constant solid phase reference (Fredlund & Rahardjo, 1993).

💧 Part IV — Soil Water Content: Quantifying the Liquid Phase

Water content is the dynamic variable of the mass-volume framework — unlike density and porosity, which change slowly over seasons, water content can change dramatically in hours (a rainstorm) or minutes (irrigation). Four standard expressions quantify it, each suited to different applications.

1. Gravimetric Water Content (w) — The Laboratory Standard

w = Mw / Ms   (dimensionless ratio, often expressed as % by multiplying × 100)

The ratio of water mass to oven-dry soil mass. Determined by the thermogravimetric method: weigh a wet sample (Mwet), oven-dry at 105°C for 24 hours, weigh again (Ms), calculate Mw = Mwet − Ms. This is the most fundamental and historically the first standardized water content measurement — simple, inexpensive, and universally accepted as the reference against which all other methods are calibrated (Gardner, 1986).

Range: 0 (oven-dry) to 0.05–0.10 (dry sandy soil) to 0.20–0.35 (moist loam) to 0.50–1.50 (saturated organic soil). Gravimetric water content can exceed 1.0 (>100%) in highly organic soils like peat, because the water mass exceeds the solid mass — a fact that often surprises students.

2. Volumetric Water Content (θ) — The Practical Standard

θ = Vw / Vt   (units: m³/m³ or cm³/cm³, dimensionless)

The volume of water per unit total volume of soil. Volumetric water content is the most widely used expression in modern soil physics, hydrology, and irrigation science because: (1) it directly indicates the depth of water stored per unit depth of soil (e.g., θ = 0.30 means 30 cm of water per 100 cm of soil depth), (2) it is directly measured by electromagnetic sensors — Time Domain Reflectometry (TDR), Frequency Domain Reflectometry (FDR), and capacitance probes — that measure soil dielectric permittivity (which depends primarily on water content because water has a dielectric constant of ~80 vs. ~3–5 for mineral solids and ~1 for air), and (3) it has a natural upper bound equal to porosity P (Topp et al., 1980).

Range: 0 (oven-dry) to P (fully saturated). At field capacity (the water content 24–48 hours after thorough wetting, when free drainage has ceased): θ ≈ 0.15–0.35 depending on texture. At permanent wilting point (the water content at which plants can no longer extract water — conventionally defined at −1.5 MPa matric potential): θ ≈ 0.05–0.25 (Brady & Weil, 2017).

3. The Critical Conversion: w ↔ θ

Gravimetric and volumetric water contents are related through bulk density:

θ = w × (ρb / ρw)   or equivalently   w = θ × (ρw / ρb)

This conversion is used daily in soil science laboratories worldwide. Since ρw ≈ 1.0 g/cm³, the simplified form is θ ≈ w × ρb. For example: a soil with w = 0.20 (20% gravimetric) and ρb = 1.35 g/cm³ has θ = 0.20 × 1.35 = 0.27 (27% volumetric). Without bulk density, the conversion is impossible — which is why ρb is measured alongside every water content determination in a properly conducted soil survey (Hillel, 2004).

4. Degree of Saturation (S) — The Pore-Filling Ratio

S = Vw / Vp = θ / P   (range: 0 to 1, or 0% to 100%)

The fraction of pore space occupied by water. S = 0 means bone dry (all pores filled with air). S = 1 means fully saturated (all pores filled with water — no air — below the water table). In the unsaturated zone (vadose zone), S typically ranges from 0.3 to 0.9. Degree of saturation connects the water content directly to the aeration status: Fa = P(1 − S) — so when S > 0.90, the air-filled porosity drops below the critical 10% threshold (Jury & Horton, 2004).

5. Liquid Ratio (ϑ) — The Shrink-Swell Variable

ϑ = Vw / Vs

Water volume relative to solid volume. This parameter is essential for characterizing expansive (vertisol) soils — the high-smectite clay soils found across the Chelif Valley, the Hodna basin, and parts of the Constantine plains in Algeria — where total volume changes with water content. Because Vs is constant, ϑ tracks water changes without the confounding effect of volume change that complicates θ in shrink-swell soils (Fredlund & Rahardjo, 1993).

📝 Part VI — Worked Example: Integrating All Concepts

A soil sample is collected from an irrigated wheat field near Sétif, Algeria using a core sampler (cylinder volume = 100 cm³). The following measurements are obtained:

MeasurementValue
Mass of wet soil + cylinder325.0 g
Mass of empty cylinder150.0 g
Mass of oven-dry soil + cylinder (after 24h at 105°C)290.0 g
Cylinder volume (Vt)100 cm³
Assumed particle density (ρs)2.65 g/cm³

Step 1 — Basic masses:
Mass of wet soil: Mwet = 325.0 − 150.0 = 175.0 g
Mass of oven-dry soil: Ms = 290.0 − 150.0 = 140.0 g
Mass of water: Mw = 175.0 − 140.0 = 35.0 g

Step 2 — Bulk density:
ρb = Ms / Vt = 140.0 / 100 = 1.40 g/cm³

Step 3 — Gravimetric water content:
w = Mw / Ms = 35.0 / 140.0 = 0.25 (25%)

Step 4 — Volumetric water content:
θ = w × ρb / ρw = 0.25 × 1.40 / 1.0 = 0.35 (35%)

Step 5 — Total porosity:
P = 1 − (ρb / ρs) = 1 − (1.40 / 2.65) = 1 − 0.528 = 0.472 (47.2%)

Step 6 — Void ratio:
e = P / (1 − P) = 0.472 / 0.528 = 0.894

Step 7 — Degree of saturation:
S = θ / P = 0.35 / 0.472 = 0.742 (74.2%)

Step 8 — Air-filled porosity:
Fa = P − θ = 0.472 − 0.35 = 0.122 (12.2%)

Step 9 — Interpretation: The air-filled porosity (12.2%) is just above the critical 10% threshold — the soil is adequately but marginally aerated. The degree of saturation (74.2%) indicates the soil is approaching field capacity. Further irrigation without drainage would push Fa below 10%, risking anaerobiosis. The bulk density (1.40 g/cm³) is within the normal range for cultivated loam but suggests some compaction — values below 1.30 would indicate better structure. This is exactly the kind of analysis an agronomist on the Sétif high plateaus would conduct to make irrigation decisions.

⚠️ Common Misconceptions

1. « Sandy soils have higher porosity than clay soils. » False. The opposite is usually true: well-structured clay soils have total porosities of 0.50–0.65, while sandy soils have 0.35–0.45. The confusion arises because sandy soils drain faster, creating the impression of more pore space — but what they have is more macropores and fewer total pores.

2. « Bulk density is a fixed property of a soil type. » False. Bulk density varies enormously with management: the same soil can have ρb = 1.1 g/cm³ after deep tillage and ρb = 1.6 g/cm³ after heavy vehicle traffic. Particle density is relatively fixed; bulk density is not (Hamza & Anderson, 2005).

3. « Gravimetric water content cannot exceed 100%. » False. In organic soils (peat, histosols), where Mw > Ms, gravimetric water content routinely exceeds 100% and can reach 500–1,500% in Sphagnum peat bogs.

4. « Volumetric and gravimetric water contents are interchangeable. » False. They differ by the factor ρb/ρw. A soil with w = 0.20 could have θ = 0.24 (if ρb = 1.2) or θ = 0.34 (if ρb = 1.7). Failing to specify which expression is being used has caused serious errors in irrigation scheduling and hydrological modelling.

5. « Porosity alone determines soil drainage. » False. Pore size distribution matters far more than total porosity. A clay soil with P = 0.55 drains poorly because its pores are predominantly micropores held by capillary forces. A sandy soil with P = 0.40 drains rapidly because its macropores empty under gravity.

6. « Air mass contributes to total soil mass. » Negligible. Air density is approximately 1.2 kg/m³ — roughly 1,000 times lighter than water and 2,200 times lighter than mineral solids. In a 100 cm³ soil sample with 20% air-filled porosity, air mass ≈ 0.024 g, versus water mass ≈ 30 g and solid mass ≈ 130 g.

🧩 Summary: The Quantitative Foundation

The mass-volume relations of soil are not abstract mathematical constructions — they are the quantitative language through which soil scientists, agronomists, hydrologists, and civil engineers communicate about the physical state of the most complex material at the Earth's surface. From Karl Terzaghi's phase diagrams in the 1920s to Daniel Hillel's comprehensive soil physics in the 2000s, this framework has proven remarkably durable because it captures the essential truth about soil: it is a three-phase system whose behaviour is governed by the proportions and interactions of its solid, liquid, and gaseous components.

Every irrigation schedule in the Mitidja Plain, every drainage design in the Chelif Valley, every foundation calculation for infrastructure in the Saharan cities, every contamination risk assessment for industrial sites — all begin with measuring ρb, ρs, w, θ, P, and S. Master these six parameters and their interconversions, and you have mastered the quantitative foundation upon which the entire edifice of soil physics is constructed.
